## Why this document exists

Sarah brought a warning to the call from her own experience. She paid for a directory listing years
ago, gathered reviews, sent clients to it — and got **zero** business from it. Her words:
*"I was very disappointed in putting any money into that."*

She is now the person recruiting graduates into this one. If the pitch oversells, she will spend the
next year absorbing that disappointment personally, from people she has relationships with.

So the discipline here is **status accuracy**: describe what the product is today, name what is
coming without promising when, and never substitute enthusiasm for a reason.

## The spine

### One line

> **It's a high-tech business card.**

Jonathan's phrase on the call; Sarah adopted it unprompted — *"that's a really great way to describe
it for people."* Lead with it. It sizes the thing honestly and it is memorable.

### The five claims — all currently true

1. **You get a professional profile page you don't have to build.** Paste a bio you already have and
   it writes the page for you — headline, "who I help", specialties. You edit anything, or regenerate it.
2. **You can take payments without setting up a merchant account.** For anyone who doesn't already
   have a way to take a credit card, that removes the whole stack.
3. **It's a place to aggregate the things that are scattered right now** — your booking link, your
   offerings, and soon your case studies and testimonials.
4. **It complements your website, it doesn't replace it.** If you already have a site you're invested
   in, this is another door into it. Every link on your profile points *out* to you.
5. **You're listed alongside other vetted HHE graduates.** The credential is the filter.

### The two-audience line

> *"If you need a shingle, this is your shingle. If you already have one, this is just another way to
> be listed."*

Use it early. It defuses the most common objection before it's raised.

## Guardrails — what not to say

| Don't say | Say instead | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "This will bring you clients" | "There's no traffic promise right now. Amy plans to promote the directory once we're out of beta — but I'm not going to tell you a number, because I don't have one." | This is the exact claim Sarah was burned by. Never make it. |
| "It's live" | "Profiles are live and public. Payments are still being wired." | Don't over-launch. Precision costs nothing and buys everything on the next call. |
| "Your 90-day trial starts now" | "Nothing is being billed yet. The $49/mo and the 90 days are what we've landed on, but the clock hasn't started — and you'll know before it does." | The clock is deliberately not running during the pilot. |
| "It's free" | "It's free while we're in pilot." | Different claim. The first one you have to walk back. |
| "It's going to be huge / a game-changer" | *(omit)* | Superlatives about our own work read as performative and cost credibility with exactly the audience that has been pitched before. |
| "You just need to…" | *(cut the softener)* | "Just" and "simply" minimize the other person's effort for them. |

**The test before any claim leaves your mouth:** *if this turns out not to happen, would I be
comfortable with them quoting it back to me?* If not, it's a plan, not a claim — say so.

## Objection handling

**"I've done a directory before and got nothing out of it."**
Sarah should tell her own story here — it's the strongest asset in the pitch, and it's hers.
Then: *"That's exactly why I'm not going to tell you it'll bring you clients. What I can tell you is
what it does today, and it's under $50 a month when it does start billing."*

**"Why do I need this? I have a website."**
*"You probably don't need the page part. What you might use is the payment side, or just being listed
where people are looking for HHE graduates specifically. Everything on your profile links back out
to your site — it's not trying to keep anyone here."*

**"What does it cost, and when?"**
*"$49 a month, with a 90-day trial. Neither has started. You'll get notice before it does."*

**"Why is it asking for my ID and my Social?"**
*"That's the payment processor, not us — same identity check any merchant account runs. Have your
driver's license or passport handy before you start that step; it takes about five minutes."*
(Sarah had to leave a call to find hers. Warn people up front.)

**"Should I rewrite my bio to sound more HHE?"**
*"No — lead with the work that fits, but don't hide the rest. The people finding this site aren't
all HHE students."* ⚠️ Pending Amy's sign-off (§D4) — flag it as your read, not policy.

**"How many people are on it?"**
Say the real number. It's a pilot; that's not a weakness unless it's hidden.

## Practical asks to make of every candidate

Keep these modest and specific — they're setup steps, not homework:

1. A **portrait photo** where you're fully in frame. *(spec + crop tool coming — don't ask until it ships)*
2. **Two or three offerings, not six.** Your gateway offers. More than that clutters the page and
   confuses buyers.
3. **A booking link** — Calendly, Acuity, Cal.com, whatever you already use. A free Calendly is fine.
4. **A working email address.** Sarah's own address on file was dead; assume others are too.

### Explaining the two CTAs — the line to use

> Your **booking link** is for people who already know they want you — referrals, your own audience,
> repeat clients. Your **offerings** are for people browsing the directory who've never heard of you.
> That's why the description matters: it's doing the work you'd otherwise do on a call.

Practitioners will ask why both exist and what to write in each. This is the answer, and it also
explains why a thin one-line offering description underperforms.
